Drama Study: A Doll's House by Henrik Ibsen

"There is no one who gives up honour for love." Henrik Ibsen, notes toward A Doll's House, 1878

A Doll's House (Et dukkehjem) was first performed at the Royal Theatre in Copenhagen on 21 December 1879. Its premiere was scandalous: Ibsen had placed a woman at the centre of a serious drama, and then allowed her to walk out of her marriage without sentimentality or punishment. The play was immediately controversial across Europe and continues to provoke argument today over what exactly Nora's departure means — whether it is a feminist manifesto, a humanist crisis, or something that resists both labels.

The play is set entirely within the Helmer family apartment over the course of three days in the week before Christmas. Nora Helmer, wife of the newly promoted bank manager Torvald, is concealing a secret: years earlier, to save her husband's life, she forged her dying father's signature to secure a loan from a man named Krogstad. She has spent years secretly repaying the debt. When Krogstad's position at the bank is threatened by Torvald's promotions, he uses this knowledge to blackmail her. What begins as a domestic comedy about a charming, frivolous wife darkens rapidly into a study of deception, identity, and the cost of self-suppression.

The major themes of the play — the performance of gender, the gap between appearance and reality, the individual's right to self-realisation, and the violence done by social convention — are rich territory for IB study. A Doll's House rewards analysis of both its realist dramatic form and its enduring global resonance.

1.

The Doll's House

"Is that my little lark twittering out there?" Torvald Helmer, Act One

Act One establishes the world of the Helmer apartment with deceptive warmth. It is Christmas Eve, the rooms are being decorated, and Nora returns from shopping loaded with packages. The scene reads, at first, as a portrait of bourgeois contentment. Torvald indulges his wife, teases her about her sweet tooth, and addresses her with a succession of diminutives — his lark, his squirrel, his little spendthrift. Ibsen presents this not as mere domestic colour but as the play's central structure of meaning: the Helmer household is a performance, and Nora is its most accomplished actress.

Into this carefully maintained surface, Ibsen introduces Christine Linde, an old friend who arrives destitute and seeking work. Christine functions as a foil: she has experienced the world without a husband's protection, and her matter-of-fact pragmatism throws Nora's sheltered existence into relief. It is to Christine that Nora confides her great secret — the forged loan she secured from Krogstad to fund Torvald's recovery trip to Italy years before. Nora treats this revelation with something close to pride. She has, in secret, acted boldly and independently. But the audience's sense of the danger she has created deepens when Krogstad himself appears and makes clear he will use his knowledge of the forgery if his position at the bank is threatened.

Ibsen's dramatic ironies in Act One operate with great precision. Every moment of warmth in the Helmer household is shadowed by what Nora conceals. The Christmas tree, brought in and decorated during this act, is a useful symbol to pursue: in Act Two it will reappear stripped and with its candles burnt down.

In Context Under Norwegian law in 1879, a married woman had no independent legal standing. She could not sign contracts, open bank accounts, or borrow money without her husband's authorisation. Nora's forgery was not merely dishonest but legally impossible for a woman to do legitimately. Ibsen was writing at a moment when women's legal and social status was beginning to be publicly contested across Scandinavia; the Norwegian Society for Women's Rights was founded the same year the play premiered. However, Ibsen himself insisted that the play was not about women's rights specifically, but about human rights: "I am not even sure what Women's Liberation really is. To me it has seemed a problem of humanity in general."

Learner Portfolio: The Architecture of Performance

In Act One, Nora performs a version of herself for almost every character she encounters: the playful, impulsive wife for Torvald; the confident, worldly woman for Christine; the frightened debtor for Krogstad. Map these performances. For each significant interaction in Act One, identify what Nora projects, what she conceals, and what the gap between the two reveals about the social pressures the play is diagnosing. Consider: is there any moment in Act One where Nora is not performing? What would it look like if she weren't?

2.

The Unraveling

"When a man has forgiven his wife, it makes him love her all the more tenderly, as it were. It is as if she had become his property in a double sense." Nils Krogstad, Act Two

Act Two opens the following day, Christmas Day, and the mood has shifted. The tree is there — stripped, its candles burnt out. Nora is increasingly desperate as Krogstad's threat becomes more serious. Torvald has not only refused to reinstate him at the bank but has sent him a letter of dismissal. Krogstad's response is to leave an incriminating letter in the Helmers' mailbox, detailing Nora's forgery. Torvald controls the only key.

Nora's attempts to intercede — with Torvald, with Christine, and in a last desperate appeal to family friend Dr. Rank — all fail. What is revealed through this act is the degree to which Nora has no agency within the systems that govern her life. She cannot ask Torvald directly because that would require telling the truth; she cannot appeal to Rank without his declaration of love turning the conversation in a direction she cannot use; she can only delay, and she does so through the only tool available to her — performance.

The tarantella is Act Two's centrepiece and one of the most discussed moments in the play. Nora insists on rehearsing the Neapolitan dance she will perform at a neighbour's party the following evening, and she dances it for Torvald with a wildness that alarms him. Ibsen stages this as simultaneously genuine desperation, calculated distraction (keeping Torvald from the mailbox), and something that exceeds either: a woman dancing at the edge of her own dissolution. The stage direction reads that she dances as if her life depends on it. It does.

In Context Ibsen developed his realist dramatic method in explicit opposition to the well-made play (pièce bien faite) dominant in European theatre at the time, a form associated with the French playwright Eugène Scribe. Well-made plays relied on elaborate plotting, misplaced documents, and a final revelation that restored social order. Ibsen retained the machinery — there is, after all, a letter in a locked box — but turned it toward purposes Scribe never intended. The revelation in Act Three does not restore order; it destroys it. The letter is not a device to resolve the plot but to expose the play's true subject, which is not the forgery but the marriage.

3.

The Slamming Door

"I have existed merely to perform tricks for you, Torvald. But you would have it so. You and Father have committed a great sin against me. It is your fault that I have made nothing of my life." Nora Helmer, Act Three

Act Three is one of the most formally audacious conclusions in modern drama. The action begins at the neighbours' party: Christine and Krogstad, who have a prior history, reach an understanding, and Krogstad resolves to withdraw his threat. But Christine stops him — not to protect Nora, but because she believes Torvald must know the truth for the marriage to have any honesty in it. The letter stays in the box.

When Torvald finally reads Krogstad's letter, his reaction is immediate and total: he turns on Nora. He is finished, ruined, destroyed — it is all about him. He strips her of any role in the children's upbringing and declares the marriage will continue as a façade for appearances' sake. Then a second letter arrives: Krogstad, moved by Christine, has returned the bond and withdrawn his threat. Torvald's reversal is instantaneous. He forgives her, calls her his little lost bird, and prepares to take her back entirely.

Nora has been watching. This is the moment she has lived in dread of — the "miracle" she hoped might come: that Torvald would prove himself equal to standing by her, taking the scandal onto himself. He has not. What follows is the play's great confrontation: Nora, methodical and clear-eyed, sits down with her husband and proceeds to dismantle eight years of marriage in a conversation conducted with the calm of someone who has finally decided to stop performing. She leaves. The door slams. The curtain falls.

In Context The contemporary reception of Act Three's ending was so hostile in Germany that Ibsen was pressured by a theatre company to write an alternative conclusion in which Nora, confronted with the sight of her sleeping children at the threshold, cannot bring herself to leave. Ibsen called it a "barbaric outrage" committed against his play and authorised the alternative only to prevent a worse unauthorised version. Reviewers across Europe debated whether Nora's departure was moral, natural, or even possible. The play's ending remains contested: some productions have staged it as tragedy, others as liberation, and others have refused to resolve it either way. Where you land on that question is, itself, an interpretive act worth examining.

Towards Assessment: Individual Oral

Supported by an extract from one non-literary text and one from a literary work, students offer a prepared response of 10 minutes, followed by 5 minutes of questions, to the following prompt: Examine the ways in which the global issue of your choice is presented through the content and form of two of the texts you have studied. (40 marks)

A Doll's House is a strong IO text. Its central tensions — between individual freedom and social expectation, between public performance and private selfhood, between what is said and what is meant — translate readily into global issues with local relevance, wide impact, and transnational reach. Two suggested pairings follow, but your own programme of study should guide your final choice.

Field of Inquiry: Culture, Identity and Community · Global Issue: How social conventions suppress individual identity

In A Doll's House, Ibsen dramatises how the social role of wife and mother colonises Nora's selfhood so thoroughly that she has, by Act Three, no identity separate from her performance of femininity. The play's form — claustrophobically domestic, three unities largely intact — enacts the confinement it diagnoses. A powerful body of work to pair with this is the collection of Dorothea Lange's Depression-era portraits, in which the social conditions of poverty overwrite individual identity in ways visible in posture, gaze, and the relation of subject to space. Alternatively, this issue pairs productively with Charlotte Perkins Gilman's "The Yellow Wallpaper", in which domestic confinement operates as both literal and psychological suppression, and the narrator's dissolution mirrors, in extremis, what Ibsen stages with more restraint.

Field of Inquiry: Power and Privilege · Global Issue: How economic dependence structures intimate relationships

Nora's situation is inseparable from her economic position: she has no income, no legal standing, and no capacity to act in the world except through Torvald's authority. Her secret loan is not only an act of love but an act of economic desperation in a system that gives her no legitimate means. This issue travels well across cultures and periods: a compelling non-literary pairing would be a curated selection of ILO or UN Women data visualisations on the gender pay gap and women's financial independence globally, read as a body of work constructing an argument through form and selection.
